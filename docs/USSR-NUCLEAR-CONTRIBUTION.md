## What was built

### Tsar Bomba (October 30, 1961) — the largest device ever detonated

Developed under Igor Kurchatov's program, with the weapon's physics package led by Yulii Khariton and
a design team including **Andrei Sakharov**, Victor Adamsky, Yuri Babayev, Yuri Smirnov, and Yuri
Trutnev. Tested over Mityushikha Bay, Novaya Zemlya, in the Arctic, dropped from 34,000 feet, detonated
by barometric fuze at roughly 13,000 feet.

**Actual yield: approximately 50 megatons** — roughly 1,570 times the combined yield of the Hiroshima
and Nagasaki bombs, about ten times the total explosive output of every weapon used in World War II.
The mushroom cloud reached over 60 km in height; the detonation flash was visible from 1,000 km away;
window glass reportedly broke at similarly extreme distances.

**A deliberate, documented act of restraint sits inside this number.** The design as originally
specified included a natural-uranium tamper (a U-238 casing around the fusion secondary) that would
have fissioned under the fusion reaction's fast neutrons, roughly doubling the yield to **over 100
megatons** — and, critically, producing the overwhelming majority of the weapon's fallout, since fast
fission of the uranium tamper is what generates most of a thermonuclear weapon's radioactive byproduct.
Soviet leadership, aware that most of that fallout would land on populated Soviet territory and
concerned about the international reaction to a 100-megaton test, ordered the uranium tamper replaced
with **lead** before the actual test. The substitution cut the yield roughly in half and eliminated an
estimated 97% of the fallout that the full design would have produced. Tsar Bomba as detonated derived
about 97% of its yield from fusion rather than fission — making it, perversely, one of the
"cleanest" high-yield nuclear detonations ever conducted, by explicit design choice, not accident.

The lesson worth extracting for this book: even the single largest nuclear detonation in history was a
scaled-down version of what its own designers were capable of building, cut back specifically to reduce
harm to the people who ordered it. Restraint and escalation happened in the same program, sometimes in
the same weapon.

**Tsar Bomba was not the ceiling of what the Soviet program built.** Khrushchev had called for a
100-megaton demonstration weapon before Tsar Bomba's test — reportedly after foreign-press speculation
about gigaton-range bombs reached the Arzamas-16 design bureau in May 1960, plausibly the same
rumor-space Edward Teller's Gnomon/Sundial pitch was circulating in on the American side that same year
(see the US document). Soviet engineers built **two additional AN602 physics packages rated for 100+
megatons** — twice Tsar Bomba's tested yield — intended for the **8F117** warhead on the **UR-500
"Gerkules" ICBM**. The missile itself was successfully tested; the nuclear-armed version was never
deployed. Unlike Sundial, which never existed as hardware, this is a case of a >100-megaton weapon
actually being built and then not fielded — restraint applied one step later in the process than on the
American side.

### Sakharov's brake — a real, well-documented case, not a dramatic-but-unverified one

Worth contrasting directly with the Teller/Sundial story in the US document, because the shapes are not
the same. Sakharov's opposition to atmospheric testing was not a single moment of conscience under social
pressure — it was a multi-year, quantified position, developed while he remained one of the program's
central weapons designers.

- **1953-1955:** using available biological data on radiocarbon uptake, Sakharov calculated that a
  single 1-megaton "clean" thermonuclear test would produce enough long-lived carbon-14 to cause an
  estimated **6,600 deaths worldwide over the following 8,000 years** — a specific quantified harm
  estimate, not a general unease.
- **1955, after the RDS-37 test:** at the celebratory banquet, Sakharov made an unscripted toast hoping
  their devices would thereafter detonate only over test ranges and never over cities. A senior military
  officer present rebuked him sharply and publicly for the remark. Sakharov later wrote that the exchange
  changed how he thought about the weapons work he was doing.
- **Following years:** Sakharov wrote and campaigned against continued atmospheric testing specifically
  on genetic/fallout grounds, work generally credited as contributing to the **1963 Partial (Limited)
  Test Ban Treaty**, which banned atmospheric, space, and underwater nuclear tests.

The contrast worth stating plainly: Teller's Sundial pitch and retreat is, on the documentary record, a
weaker story than its popular version — personal pique, no evidence of a real-time retreat under
pressure. Sakharov's brake is the stronger story, extensively documented, sustained over years, and tied
to an actual treaty outcome. If this book wants one clean case of an individual scientist's restraint
producing observable institutional consequence, it's this one, not Sundial.

### Perimeter ("Dead Hand") — automated retaliation authority, and directly on-theme for this book

Developed 1974-1985, formally the **Perimeter** system (Периметр); "Dead Hand" is a Western label popularized
by David E. Hoffman's 2009 book of that name, not the Soviet or Russian designation. Worth including
here specifically because it is the clearest historical precedent for this book's central concern:
**at what point does launch authority stop requiring a specific human being who is alive, informed, and
present to exercise it.**

**What it was not:** a fully autonomous machine authorized to independently detect attack and launch
retaliation without any human decision in the loop — the popular "doomsday machine" framing overstates
the design.

**What it was:** an emergency communications backstop, built on the fear that a decapitating first
strike could kill Soviet leadership and destroy normal command-and-control before retaliation could be
ordered. If Perimeter's sensors indicated a nuclear detonation on Soviet soil and normal command
channels had gone silent, the system's role was to transfer launch *authority* to a duty officer deep in
a hardened bunker — a human being, but possibly a comparatively junior one, operating on the
system's inference that leadership was already dead, rather than on direct confirmation. If activated,
Perimeter could launch specialized **command missiles** that would overfly Soviet territory broadcasting
launch codes to the remaining arsenal, allowing retaliation even if every other layer of the chain of
command had been destroyed.

The precise degree to which Perimeter remains operational, and the precise degree of human discretion
retained by the duty officer at the bottom of that chain, are not fully public. What is well-documented
is the design intent: preserve the capacity to retaliate under conditions where the normal human
decision-makers are presumed dead, by pushing the final human decision as far down and as automated-up
as the system's architects were willing to go. That is a real, deployed historical instance of exactly
the Band IV question this book asks about AI systems — not an analogy, a direct precedent, built by
humans, for reasons that made sense to the humans who built it, decades before anyone was asking the
question about machine autonomy instead of institutional autonomy.

## What was not built

### The full-yield Tsar Bomba (100+ Mt) — see above

Worth restating in this section explicitly: the 100-megaton, uranium-tampered version of Tsar Bomba was
a real, engineered design, not a hypothetical — and it was not built. What was tested was the
deliberately reduced version.

### The cobalt bomb — Szilard's warning, never realized by anyone

A "salted" weapon, jacketed in cobalt-59, which upon detonation would convert into cobalt-60 — a gamma
emitter with a roughly 5.27-year half-life — and disperse it as long-lived fallout over a wide area.

The concept originates not from a weapons program but from a warning: physicist **Leo Szilard**
described it in a radio broadcast on **February 26, 1950**, explicitly *not* to propose it be built, but
to demonstrate that nuclear weapons technology was approaching the point where a single design decision
could plausibly end human life on Earth — the original conceptual "doomsday device." Szilard spent much
of the rest of his life campaigning against salted weapons of this kind. Despite decades of subsequent
speculation (amplified by its use as the plot device in *Dr. Strangelove*), **there is no confirmed
evidence a cobalt bomb, Soviet or American, has ever been built or tested.**

Paired with Sundial on the American side (see the companion document), this is the cleanest available
case of a weapon that stopped at the whiteboard specifically because the people who understood its
implications judged it should.
