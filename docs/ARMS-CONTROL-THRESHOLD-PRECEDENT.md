# A Real Precedent for Chapter VI: The 1974 Threshold Test Ban Treaty

**Status:** Background/grounding document. Flagging this because it's not analogy — it's a documented
historical case of two adversarial nuclear powers doing the specific thing Chapter VI ("Thresholds as
Attachment Points") argues AI governance needs to do: converting a vague commitment into a named,
numeric, mutually observable line neither side crosses.

---

## What it is

The **Treaty on the Limitation of Underground Nuclear Weapon Tests**, signed by the US and USSR in July
1974, generally known as the **Threshold Test Ban Treaty (TTBT)**. It prohibits either country from
testing an underground nuclear weapon with a yield exceeding **150 kilotons**.

That's the whole mechanism. Not a ban on testing, not a ban on weapons development — a specific numeric
ceiling, applied to underground tests only (atmospheric, space, and underwater tests were already
banned under the 1963 Partial Test Ban Treaty, driven substantially by the fallout-and-genetic-damage
case Sakharov built — see [USSR-NUCLEAR-CONTRIBUTION.md](./USSR-NUCLEAR-CONTRIBUTION.md)).

## Why it's a better precedent than it looks

The 150-kiloton line is militarily meaningful in exactly the way this book's own thresholds are supposed
to be: it removes the ability to test new or upgraded weapons designs above the fractional-megaton range,
without banning weapons development outright or requiring either side to trust the other's intentions —
only its adherence to an observable, checkable number.

**Verification was imperfect, and both sides said so up front rather than pretending otherwise.** Both
governments acknowledged in 1974 that measurement uncertainty made occasional unintentional overshoot
plausible, and explicitly agreed that one or two unintentional breaches per year would not constitute a
treaty violation — while preserving either side's right to investigate a suspected breach. That's a
threshold with a stated tolerance band and an explicit dispute-resolution mechanism attached, not a
brittle bright line that breaks the whole agreement on the first ambiguous reading.

**Ratification lagged implementation.** The TTBT wasn't formally ratified by either side until 1990,
alongside a companion treaty on peaceful nuclear explosions — but both countries announced in 1976 that
they would observe the 150-kiloton limit regardless. Worth noting for Chapter VI's argument that a
threshold doesn't need to wait for a fully ratified treaty to start doing governance work — a credible,
publicly stated, mutually observed commitment can function before the formal instrument exists.

**Not retroactive, and the discontinuity at the observance date is worth seeing clearly.** Before 1976,
megaton-range underground tests were routine on both sides — the US test **Cannikin** (Nov 6, 1971,
Amchitka Island) was an underground detonation of nearly 5 megatons, the largest underground test the US
ever conducted (testing the W71 warhead for the Spartan ABM interceptor; the public campaign against it
is the direct origin of Greenpeace). Two 1973 Soviet tests at Novaya Zemlya totaled 7.8 megatons
underground. The 1975 Soviet test series still peaked at 1.3 megatons. The 1976 series — the first year
of declared observance — peaked at 130 kilotons, under the cap. That's a genuine behavioral discontinuity
at the declared threshold, not a gradual taper, which is exactly the kind of observable signature this
book's own ladder is designed to look for.

**Compliance after that point was real but contested, not clean — and this is the part most relevant to
Chapter III's "no equation of state" argument.** US intelligence periodically suspected Soviet tests
exceeded 150kt; a Reagan-administration report on Soviet arms-control noncompliance raised this formally.
The underlying problem was largely measurement, not necessarily intent: yield estimation from seismic
data at the Semipalatinsk test site carried roughly a **factor-of-two uncertainty**, because of how the
local geology coupled the shockwave — a genuine 150kt test could read as anywhere from 75kt to 300kt
depending on the correction model applied. Some tests that initially appeared to breach the threshold by
a wide margin were later reclassified as compliant once the geological bias was corrected for; new tests
then reopened the same ~2x discrepancy within a year. No violation was ever confirmed on the public
record, and the suspicion was never fully resolved either — it stayed a live, unsettled verification
dispute for years.

**Why that matters here specifically:** a real, mutually intended, numerically explicit threshold — the
clean case, in principle — still ran into exactly the epistemic problem this book's Chapter III argues AI
thresholds will face in a harder form. The number is only as good as the ability to measure which side of
it an actual event landed on, and here that measurement carried a 2x error bar baked into the physics of
the test site's rock. A threshold with no measurement noise is a policy ideal; TTBT is the historical
case showing what a real one, with real noise, actually looks like in practice — imperfect, argued over,
and still functionally better than having no threshold at all.

## What it doesn't cover — the gap that stayed informal

No treaty, before or since, has ever named cobalt or "salted" weapons specifically (see
[US-NUCLEAR-CONTRIBUTION.md](./US-NUCLEAR-CONTRIBUTION.md) and
[USSR-NUCLEAR-CONTRIBUTION.md](./USSR-NUCLEAR-CONTRIBUTION.md) for that history). The restraint that kept
either side from building one stayed tacit — plausibly reinforced by the general fallout-focused thrust
of the 1963 and 1974 treaties, but never codified as its own rule. Worth holding both facts side by side:
an explicit numeric threshold (TTBT) worked and was durably observed for sixteen years pre-ratification;
an informal, unstated taboo (salted weapons) also worked, for different and less legible reasons nobody
can fully audit. This book's own Rule 1 — observable facts, not inferred intentions — has an obvious
preference for the first kind, and this is the historical case that preference is built on.
