# II. What Kahn Did, and What He Got Wrong

**Status:** Draft 2026-09-09 (non-normative prose; bracketed items need sourcing).

## The four moves worth stealing

Kahn's method, stripped of his conclusions, is four moves. This book
steals all four and argues with the man everywhere else.

**Resolution.** Before Kahn, nuclear outcomes came in one size: the end.
His 1960 *On Thermonuclear War* - six hundred pages from a RAND physicist
who had spent the 1950s inside war-gaming rooms - insisted outcomes come
in sizes, and that sizes can be tabulated: yields, fatalities, industrial
survival, recovery timelines, scenario by scenario. The offense his
critics never forgave was granularity itself. It is also the whole of
this book's Chapter IV method: a rung nobody can distinguish from its
neighbors is not a rung, it's mood.

**Named thresholds.** In *On Escalation* (1965) Kahn drew a ladder with
forty-four rungs - the count this book mirrors deliberately - and gave
the important transitions names: thresholds with examples attached, so
that a president could be told not merely "things are getting worse" but
"we are about to cross this named line, and here is what changes on the
far side." Chapter VI is that move transplanted: thresholds A through E,
each with an owner of the brake.

**Tragic but distinguishable.** Kahn's ugliest and most necessary habit:
refusing to let catastrophe be one undifferentiated mass. Sixty million
dead with institutions intact is not the same as sixty million dead
without them, and policy that cannot say so cannot choose between
postures that produce one or the other. Chapter VII takes its title from
this habit.

**The aftermath as a subject.** Kahn wrote about war termination - what
the day after requires, what recovery assumes, what deterrence is for -
when respectable discourse held that planning for survival was itself
provocative. Chapter IX is that move: the day after machine-speed failure
is a subject, not an afterthought.

## What he got wrong: the numbers, then the physics

Take the method, not the conclusions - because the conclusions were
wrong in the direction his critics predicted and his method couldn't
catch. Kahn's fatality tables and recovery timelines assumed what the
models of 1960 assumed: that the damage ends when the fires go out. His
shelter advocacy - a national program on the order of thirty billion
1960 dollars - priced survival as an engineering problem with an
engineering budget `[construction: verify figure and edition]`.

Then the physics moved. TTAPS in 1983 (Turco, Toon, Ackerman, Pollack,
Sagan) showed that burning cities inject soot into the stratosphere and
the growing season fails half a world away from any target; later work
(Robock and others, 2007 onward) showed even a regional exchange does
global agricultural damage. The recovery timelines - the economy
rebuilt in years, the distinguishable aftermaths - assumed a sky that
behaves. It doesn't. Kahn's ladder survived this correction and his
timetables didn't, which is exactly the hierarchy this book adopts:
order the losses, hold the dates lightly, and let later physics revise
the columns without breaking the rows.

There is a second, smaller wrongness worth recording because this book
risks repeating it. Kahn wrote as an American strategist for American
principals, and his rungs quietly assume the reader is the one climbing
up rather than the one being climbed over. A ladder of control loss
reads differently from the dugout than from the situation room. The
sister book on hyperwar exists partly to keep that asymmetry visible.

## The counterforce objection, at full strength, unresolved

Here is the strongest case against this entire enterprise, stated as its
believers state it, because paraphrase would be theft:

*To enumerate the rungs of limited war is to make limited war thinkable,
and to make it thinkable is to make it fightable. The analyst who draws
forty-four steps between peace and ruin has built forty-four stopping
places that flatter the decision-maker - each one whispering that control
is still possible one step further down. Kahn did not describe escalation.
He equipped it. Every threshold named is a threshold someone will test,
and the taxonomy of restraint becomes the manual of transgression.*

That objection was pressed against Kahn in the 1960s debate, in print,
by serious people, and it has never been answered away `[construction:
source the strongest published version and quote it fairly - candidates
in the escalation-debate literature, not a generic formulation]`. This
chapter will not resolve it either. The honest form of the reply is
procedural, not philosophical, and it lives in this book's design
constraints: rungs as institutional facts rather than instructions,
governance criteria rather than defeat mechanisms, brakes rather than
accelerators (Chapter I, CONTRIBUTING.md). Whether procedure answers
substance is genuinely open. It is carried forward, explicitly
unresolved, into Chapter X - which is also where Kahn's own closest
analogue lives: his argument that a deterrence threshold kept secret
isn't a threshold but a surprise, and the question of whether that logic
transfers to a ladder of control loss. No loop-closing here. The tension
stays on the books.
