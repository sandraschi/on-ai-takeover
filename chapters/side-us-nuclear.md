## Why this side chapter exists

This book asks readers to take Herman Kahn seriously as a strategist. Kahn's authority came from
having done the arithmetic on real weapons designed by real people, several of which never got built
precisely because someone, somewhere, decided the arithmetic was too horrifying to act on. A reader
who doesn't know who Edward Teller was, what the Teller-Ulam design actually did, or which American
weapons concepts were abandoned before construction is missing the ground floor this book is built on.

This is the US half; see Side - The Soviet Contribution for the Soviet
half, including the Perimeter system — directly relevant to this book's theme of delegated launch
authority.

## What was built

### The Teller-Ulam design (1951) — the thing that made thermonuclear weapons possible

Fission bombs (Hiroshima, Nagasaki) split heavy atoms and release energy on the order of tens of
kilotons. A thermonuclear ("hydrogen") bomb fuses light atoms and can release energy orders of
magnitude larger, but nobody had a working design for igniting fusion at weapon scale until 1951.

Stanislaw Ulam, a mathematician, proposed a two-stage architecture: a fission "primary" whose output
compresses and ignites a separate fusion "secondary." Edward Teller — Hungarian-American physicist,
present at the Manhattan Project, obsessed with the "Super" (thermonuclear weapons) since the early
1940s — proposed using the primary's *radiation*, not its blast wave, to do the compressing. Ulam's
staging plus Teller's radiation-implosion insight together became the **Teller-Ulam design**, still the
basis of every modern thermonuclear weapon in every arsenal today. Credit allocation between the two men
remained personally and professionally contentious for the rest of their lives; the historical record
does not cleanly resolve it, and this side chapter doesn't try to.

The design was proven on **November 1, 1952**, at Enewetak Atoll, in the test codenamed **Ivy Mike** —
not a deliverable weapon, an experimental device the size of a small building, yield about 10.4
megatons, roughly 700 times Hiroshima. Deliverable, weaponized versions followed within a few years.

### The neutron bomb — built, deployed, then withdrawn

Worth stating precisely, since it's commonly misfiled alongside weapons that were never built: **the
neutron bomb (enhanced radiation weapon) was designed, manufactured, stockpiled, and deployed.** It is
not a "fortunately avoided" case — it's a "built, argued about for a decade, and later voluntarily
withdrawn" case, which is arguably the more useful precedent for this book's Chapter VIII (Descent).

- **Concept:** Samuel T. Cohen, Lawrence Livermore, 1958. A small thermonuclear device engineered to
  minimize blast/thermal yield and maximize prompt neutron radiation — designed to kill personnel
  (including inside armor) over a smaller area, with reduced blast damage to structures and reduced
  long-lived fallout compared to a standard weapon of similar size. Concept tested underground in
  Nevada in 1963.
- **Production:** two warhead types entered the US stockpile — the **W70 Mod 3** (Lance short-range
  missile, 380 units built) and the **W79 Mod 0** (8-inch artillery shell, 40 units built).
- **Political history:** the program became public via a *Washington Post* story in June 1977, sparking
  a decade of "killer warhead" controversy in Europe and the US. President Carter deferred (but did not
  cancel) production in April 1978. President Reagan restarted production in 1981; first units were
  completed that year and deployed.
- **Withdrawal:** President George H.W. Bush ordered the type retired in 1992, after the Cold War ended.
  The last W70 was dismantled in 1996; the last W79 by 2003.

The lesson worth extracting for this book: a weapon can be built, deployed, argued over for years, and
still be voluntarily dismantled once the institutional and political conditions that justified it
changed. Descent from a rung is not purely hypothetical.

### Castle Bravo (March 1, 1954) — when the designers' own physics failed

Included here even though it's a "what went wrong," not a "what was and wasn't built," because it's
directly relevant to this book's Chapter III argument (no equation of state) — and because the people
who had actually built the hydrogen bomb still got their own arithmetic wrong by a wide margin, in both
directions, in the same few weeks.

A Los Alamos design, part of the Operation Castle series at Bikini Atoll, predicted to yield 4-8
megatons. **It yielded 15 megatons** — roughly triple the top of the predicted range. Cause: the fuel
used lithium deuteride, and designers assumed the lithium-7 isotope present in the mix was essentially
inert. It wasn't — under neutron bombardment it produced tritium and an extra neutron, which fed
directly back into the fusion reaction and substantially amplified the total yield, a mechanism the
designers hadn't modeled correctly beforehand.

The consequence went well beyond an embarrassing miss: the explosion threw radioactive fallout across
roughly 7,000 square miles, contaminating the inhabited atolls of Rongelap, Utirik, and Ailinginae, and
dosing the Japanese fishing boat *Daigo Fukuryū Maru* (Lucky Dragon 5) more than 80 miles outside the
zone anyone expected to be dangerous. One crew member died from the exposure; it became a major
international incident and fed directly into the global anti-atmospheric-testing sentiment that, from
the Soviet side, Sakharov was independently building toward the same year (see
Side - The Soviet Contribution).

The same test series, within weeks, also produced the opposite failure: **Castle Koon**, a Livermore
design, fizzled to a yield well below prediction. One series, one set of designers, two failures in
opposite directions — a real historical instance of not having a reliable predictive equation even in a
domain, nuclear physics, that does fundamentally have one. The difference from the AI governance problem
this book addresses: the lithium-7 error was identifiable, correctable, and got folded into every
subsequent calculation. There was a specific wrong term to find and fix. See
Chapter III for why that convergence
path isn't available for AI capability prediction.

## What was not built

### Project Gnomon and Project Sundial (1954) — the 10-gigaton weapon that never existed as hardware

In 1954, at an Atomic Energy Commission meeting, Edward Teller reported that his Livermore lab was
working on two new weapon concepts: **Gnomon**, at roughly 1,000 megatons, intended to function as a
"primary" trigger stage for **Sundial**, at roughly **10,000 megatons (10 gigatons)** — around 200 times
the yield of Tsar Bomba, the largest device ever actually detonated (see the Soviet side chapter).

Worth being precise about the actual sequence, since the tidier version of this story (Teller pitches it
on strategic grounds, the room pushes back, he backs down) is not what the declassified GAC minutes
support. By contemporaries' account, Teller made the pitch not long after Livermore's own H-bomb design
had failed at the Castle test series — specifically **Castle Koon**, a Livermore device that fizzled to
well below its predicted yield (see the Castle Bravo entry above for the series' other, opposite failure)
— and the AEC had cancelled another of his projects. The read from people present was that he was
"feeling wounded" and "tried to raise the ante, to be the bold idea man,"
reputational one-upmanship rather than a Soviet-competition argument. **The recorded shock — Dr. Fisk
worrying it would "contaminate the earth," GAC chairman Isidor Rabi dismissing it as "an advertising
stunt, and not to be taken too seriously" — happened after Teller had already left the meeting.** There
is no record of him present for the pushback, and no record of a deliberate retreat under it; one
contemporary's assessment was that Teller stayed committed to an idea only "until the next enthusiasm
took over." Modern estimates of Sundial's effects, had it been built and detonated, describe a fireball
up to 50 km across, ignition of everything within roughly 400 km, and blast effects on the order of a
magnitude-9 earthquake, with associated nuclear-winter-scale climate effects. **Sundial was never built
or tested.** It exists only as a 1954 proposal, a walkout, and a set of reactions recorded after the
fact — the clean case, alongside the Soviet cobalt bomb (see the Soviet side chapter), of a weapon concept
that stopped at the point where the people capable of building it decided not to, for reasons that had
nothing to do with anyone changing Teller's mind in the room.
