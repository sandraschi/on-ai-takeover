# Corollary: Sundial and Tsar Bomba — A Rung Never Stepped Onto, and a Half-Rung Climbdown

**Status:** Canonical Corollary (2026-09-16). Two historical weapons programs, read as pre-history
illustrations of this book's own restraint vocabulary (Chapter VIII, "Descent") — not analogies borrowed
from outside the subject, but the same mechanism (a capability approached, then not fully taken) showing
up decades before there was an AI ladder to put it on.

---

## Two shapes of restraint, not one

This book's Chapter VIII asks what moves a civilization *down* a rung, or stops it from climbing one at
all. The nuclear arms race supplies two clean, well-documented cases of exactly that — and they are not
the same shape of restraint. Worth keeping them distinct, because the institutional preconditions for
each are different, and a governance framework that only knows how to produce one won't reliably produce
the other.

## Case 1: Sundial — the rung never stepped onto

Full history in [docs/US-NUCLEAR-CONTRIBUTION.md](../docs/US-NUCLEAR-CONTRIBUTION.md). Short version:
Edward Teller proposed a 10-gigaton (10,000-megaton) weapon design to the AEC's General Advisory
Committee in 1954, not long after his own Livermore lab's design (Castle Koon) had fizzled and another
project had been cancelled — contemporaries describe the pitch as personal one-upmanship, not strategic
argument. The room's recorded shock (Fisk: it would "contaminate the earth"; Rabi: "an advertising
stunt") happened after Teller had already left. There's no record of him retreating under pressure —
the best account is that he moved on to his next enthusiasm.

**What matters structurally, independent of Teller's motives:** Sundial never became hardware. No metal
was cut, no test was scheduled, no physics package was assembled. Zero capability was ever instantiated.
Whatever stopped it — institutional indifference, the absence of a sponsor willing to fund it, Teller's
own attention moving elsewhere — stopped it *before the first physical step*.

### How big a rung would it have been

Worth being precise rather than reaching for the largest available comparison. Sundial's 10,000-megaton
design yield is roughly two orders of magnitude below a 1-mile-class asteroid impact (~1,000,000
megatons) and roughly four orders of magnitude below the Chicxulub impactor (~100,000,000+ megatons,
estimates vary). Scaling by impactor energy, 10,000 megatons corresponds to roughly what a 300-400 meter
diameter asteroid would deliver — a serious regional-devastation event, not remotely an extinction-level
one. Worth noting separately: even a hypothetically "clean" (near-zero-fallout) Sundial would still have
caused a real global climate event through two non-radioactive mechanisms — firestorm soot lofted into
the stratosphere across its ~400km thermal-ignition radius, and ozone-layer destruction from the
fireball's own NOx thermochemistry, both mechanisms independent of fission-product fallout entirely.
"Clean" limits the radiological damage, not the blast, thermal, or climate damage.

## Case 2: Tsar Bomba — the half-rung climbdown

Full history in [docs/USSR-NUCLEAR-CONTRIBUTION.md](../docs/USSR-NUCLEAR-CONTRIBUTION.md). Short version:
the Soviet design as engineered included a natural-uranium tamper that would have roughly doubled the
yield to 100+ megatons — and, because fast fission of that tamper is what generates the overwhelming
majority of a thermonuclear weapon's fallout, would have produced vastly more radioactive contamination,
most of it landing on populated Soviet territory. Before the actual test, Soviet leadership ordered the
uranium tamper replaced with lead. The substitution cut yield roughly in half (to the ~50 megatons
actually detonated) and eliminated an estimated 97% of the fallout the full design would have produced.

**What matters structurally:** unlike Sundial, this rung *was* climbed — physically built, physically
tested, physically detonated, still the largest nuclear explosion in human history. The restraint
happened one step later in the process: not "never start," but "modify the design to step back from the
top of the rung, immediately before executing it." The 100+ megaton, full-fallout version was real
engineering, sitting in a warehouse, ready to fly, and someone made a deliberate decision to swap one
component before it did.

## Why the distinction is worth carrying into Chapter VIII

Two different institutional preconditions produce these two different shapes of restraint, and a
governance framework aimed only at one will predictably miss the other:

- **Sundial-type restraint (never occupy the rung) requires something upstream of the technical
  process** — social or professional norms strong enough that the capability is never seriously pursued
  in the first place. It's cheap when it works (no resources spent, no risk taken) and completely
  unreliable as a mechanism, because it depends on nobody with the ability to build the thing wanting to
  badly enough, for as long as the capability exists to be pursued. Teller wanting to build Sundial and
  simply losing interest is not a governance mechanism anyone should plan around; it happened to work
  once, by accident of personality.
- **Tsar Bomba-type restraint (climb most of the way, step back at the top) requires something
  *embedded in the technical process itself*** — a design decision point late enough to still catch the
  worst version of the capability, but early enough that stepping back is still physically possible
  before the consequence is locked in. This is a real, buildable governance target: a mandatory design
  review or swap-out gate positioned deliberately before the point of no return, not a hope that nobody
  wants to build the bad version.

Applied to this book's own subject: a lab that never starts a training run past a capability threshold is
doing Sundial-type restraint — valuable, but resting entirely on nobody at any lab, ever, wanting to
cross that line, for as long as the technology exists. A lab that starts a run, then deliberately
truncates it, downgrades the release checkpoint, or withholds a capability discovered mid-training is
doing Tsar Bomba-type restraint — a designed-in off-ramp exercised at a specific, identifiable decision
point, which is the kind of mechanism a threshold (see
[docs/ARMS-CONTROL-THRESHOLD-PRECEDENT.md](../docs/ARMS-CONTROL-THRESHOLD-PRECEDENT.md)) can actually be
built to require, rather than merely hope for. Chapter VIII should distinguish these explicitly rather
than treating "descent" as one undifferentiated category — the two require entirely different
institutional plumbing to produce reliably, and this book's own Rule 1 (observable acts, not inferred
intentions) has an obvious preference for the second kind, for the same reason it prefers TTBT's
explicit threshold over the informal, unenforceable taboo against salted weapons.
