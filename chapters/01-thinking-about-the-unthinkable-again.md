# I. Thinking About the Unthinkable, Again

**Status:** Draft 2026-09-09 (non-normative prose; facts cited inline).

## The binary, stated this week by people who should know better

On 2026-09-09 at 00:04Z Jacob Coxon - three years in pre-training at
Anthropic and OpenAI - posted that the people building AI earnestly
believe it could kill us all by the end of the decade, and that neither
company is acting responsibly. At 01:27Z Evan Hubinger, Alignment Science
lead at Anthropic, replied that Coxon was correct. He put his own estimate
at greater than ten percent within a decade and added two sentences that
matter more than the number: Anthropic does not have a plan to solve
alignment for superintelligence, and it is not clearly on track to find
one. By 09:11Z the exchange had passed thirty million views.

Read those posts carefully and notice what they do not contain. No
distinction between ways it goes wrong. No ranking of outcomes. No
account of what, exactly, gets lost first, or what could still be saved
after it is lost. Just the number and the abyss: ten percent, everyone,
a decade.

This is the current binary, and it comes from both sides. The labs say:
either we solve alignment and things are broadly fine, or we don't and
it's over. The critics say the same thing with the signs reversed: either
you believe the labs or you're a doomer. Both framings agree on the only
point that matters for action - that there is nothing in between worth
describing. This book exists because that agreement is the actual problem,
not a simplification of it.

## Why refusing to distinguish is abdication, not seriousness

Refusing to distinguish between bad outcomes reads as moral seriousness.
It functions as moral abdication. The mechanism is arithmetic: if every
failure mode is "everyone dies," then every policy choice is equivalent,
and equivalent choices need no thought. Pause training or accelerate it,
audit the labs or defund them, build the hotline or skip it - all of it
collapses to postures toward the abyss rather than decisions about the
world. A movement that cannot say which outcome it is trying to prevent
cannot say what it is trying to do, and a lab that cannot say what it
fears cannot say what would reassure it. Hubinger's post is the exhibit:
the owner of the safety case announces there is no plan, and the next
training run starts on schedule, because nothing in the binary tells
anyone what a plan would even attach to.

Consider what the binary cost the last time we ran this experiment. Before
Kahn, nuclear discourse had the same shape: either deterrence holds and
we are fine, or it doesn't and everyone dies, so thinking about the middle
- limited exchange, escalation ladders, civil defense, war termination -
was treated as itself dangerous, as if describing a rung built it. The
people who held that position were serious people. What it cost was
precisely the ability to act: a decade of policy made of posture, no
language for the steps between peace and ruin, and war plans that jumped
from signal to apocalypse with nothing in between. Kahn's offense was not
optimism. It was taxonomy - insisting that bad outcomes come in sizes,
that sizes can be ordered, and that ordering them is the precondition for
doing anything at all.

## The hazard, stated here and not in an appendix

A reader three chapters in will wonder why nobody has addressed the
obvious objection: doesn't describing the rungs teach people to climb
them? It deserves an answer in this chapter's own voice, not relegated
to an acknowledgements section.

The answer is the book's design constraint, and it is absolute. Every
rung in Chapter IV is an observable institutional fact - who fielded
what, when, under whose authority - plus the governance criterion that
would have stopped it. No defeat mechanisms. No tactics, no build guides,
no targeting detail, no code. A paragraph that helps an operator press
an accelerator gets cut no matter how true it is; a paragraph that helps
a principal attach a brake ships. The test is stated in CONTRIBUTING.md
and applied per rung, not per book: does this help attach a brake, or
press an accelerator? The ladder describes where control leaves. It does
not describe how to take it.

There is a second, quieter hazard worth naming: the ladder can be read
as a schedule. It isn't. Rungs are ordered by reversibility, not by date,
and nothing here predicts when - or whether - any given step falls. The
book locates the present (Chapter V, rung 13 moving to 14 as of this
week). It does not foretell the future.

## What this book does instead

If bad outcomes come in sizes, the first task is to order them by what
actually matters: reversibility of human control. Not capability - a
benchmark score cannot be dated and cannot be acted on. Not severity -
severity is what the binary already gives you. Reversibility: at each
step, who could still say no, how fast, and what happens when the answer
arrives late.

That ordering is Chapter IV: forty-four rungs in six bands, from
agent workdays (rung 6, 3.1 per human workday as of mid-August 2026)
through the audit floor the field is approaching now (Threshold B),
to terminal states that are distinct from each other and therefore
demanding distinct responses (Chapter VIII). Chapter III explains why no
equation does this work for us - Toby Ord's August 2026 result, taken
seriously, proves the curve cannot give early warning, which is why the
rungs are institutional facts instead. Chapter V dates the present.
Chapter VI names, for each threshold, the owner of the brake. Chapter VII
records the safety case failing in the owner's own words. Chapter IX
asks what the day after looks like, because Kahn taught that war
termination is part of war, and the same holds here.

The binary asks you to choose between fine and over. This book asks
something harder and more useful: to say exactly what we lose first,
and to hold that line while it can still be held.
